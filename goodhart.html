"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

Marilyn Strathern's phrasing (1997) of Charles Goodhart's 1975 observation about monetary policy.

You can rarely optimize the thing you actually care about — student learning, code quality, a model being genuinely helpful. So you optimize a proxy you can measure: test scores, tickets closed, benchmark accuracy. Goodhart's Law says the correlation you relied on breaks precisely because you leaned on it.

Manheim & Garrabrant's paper Categorizing Variants of Goodhart's Law showed this isn't one failure but four distinct mechanisms. Each tab below is one of them, simulated live: every dot is a candidate you might pick (a policy, a model, an employee, an answer), plotted by how good it looks on the metric (→) versus how good it actually is (↑). Drag the slider to optimize harder — select a smaller elite by metric score — and watch what the selection actually delivers.

Why this page lives on this site

Prompt Studio's reasoning and intent layers are gated by scored evals — 31/31 on one corpus, 42/42 on another. Those numbers are proxies, and this page is the caution label: the corpora are written by the same people who tune the thresholds, which is regressional Goodhart waiting to happen. It's also why the graph layer is tuned for precision over recall and gets a fire-rate ceiling rather than a fire-rate target — a target would invite exactly the gaming simulated above. And it's why prompts here state goals rather than forcing form: a word-cap is a proxy for succinctness, and models optimize the cap, not the succinctness.
